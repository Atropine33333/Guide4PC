# Appendix 0: To the Friend Who Walked Beside Me

> — A small note of thanks, tucked inside a technical manual, for someone who might just recognise these moments

Before I begin talking about hard drives and memory modules, about file systems and firewalls and all the cold, orderly things that live inside a machine, I need to say something about something that lives outside it — something warmer, far less orderly, and quietly important to me.

This isn't a chapter of instruction. It has no diagrams, no command lines, no troubleshooting steps. If I'm honest, it's a letter. A letter slipped between the pages of a book about computers, where a certain friend of mine might come across it one day.

I've written this guide for many people — classmates who've never owned a laptop, friends who click and hope, strangers who will download the file and forget it by morning. But this appendix is for one specific person. Someone with a tight ponytail that swings when she walks — a walk that is languor and slightly sideways.

That person is you.

You've always been a quiet person — not the shy kind of quiet, but the kind that's comfortable in silence. You speak when you have something to say, and you don't feel the need to fill every gap. I've always been more restless, more talkative, the one who starts conversations or makes jokes. And somehow, we fit. Over the years, I've learned that with you I can just be myself — no performance, no guarding. Two friends doing homework — or pretending to — in a pavilion.

I remember those PE afternoons as a different kind of peace. Tucked in the pocket park between the teaching buildings — a little bamboo grove with a wooden pavilion — we'd escape during the sports period. The morning session, at eleven, brought sun almost directly overhead, so bright you had to squint, carving sharp shadows from the roof onto your shoulders. The afternoon session, at half past three, came with slanting light through the gaps between the buildings, stretching the tree shadows and soaking the whole park in honey. While others ran and shouted, we'd slip into that pavilion with our homework and sit side by side.

We said we were doing homework. But we always ended up talking.

You talked about your cat — that long‑haired, white‑and‑orange creature you called "Juzhang." It took me a second to realise it was a pun on "bureau chief." You said it with that little upward tilt of your mouth. You told me how he hid under your bed when strangers came, how he only crept out long after the door was closed. Then one day, you said he'd crawled into a donut‑shaped box, and you'd tilted it upright, leaving just the opening. I reached my hand in — and touched a patch of warm, impossibly soft fur. He didn't scratch, didn't run. I stroked his head, behind his ears, under his chin. He started to purr, and after a while he even leaned forward to sniff my fingers. When I pulled my hand out of the box, we looked at each other and you smiled. You said he would "learn from his mistake" and never crawl in there again. That small, ordinary afternoon stayed with me, because it was a good one — the kind you have with an old friend where nothing much happens, and that's the point.

In the pavilion, we often finished only a couple of problems in an entire class, or left a whole worksheet half‑done. The sunlight moved slowly across the pages — from the tip of your pen to the back of my hand, then from my hand to the scattered pile of erasers and pen caps between us. Sometimes we'd leave the pavilion and walk a few laps on the track. One lap, two laps. The red cinder crunching under our shoes. Our shadows — bunched into small dark puddles under the eleven o'clock sun, or stretched long in the half‑past‑three light — fell side by side on the red track. 

I also remember visiting your home and listening to you play the piano. Before you sat on the bench, you'd glance at me and say, in that small, shy voice, "I'm not very good at it." Then you'd press the soft pedal with your left foot and keep it there — the one that moves the hammers closer to the strings, making the sound gentle and distant, as if from another room. You also used the sustain pedal whenever you felt the piece needed "a bit of maestoso flavour." You'd hold it down and let the notes bleed into one another, staying in the air an extra second. When you hit a wrong note, you'd stop, frown, and start again. I'd sit on the chair nearby, pretending to look at my phone, but really I wasn't looking at anything. I was listening — those hesitant, honest notes, softened and blurred by the pedals. They were yours, and I was glad to be there.

And there's something else I've come to value more than I can easily say. You've accepted my imperfections — the clumsy, not‑good‑enough parts of me that I try so hard to hide — and you've never made me feel small for them. More than that, you've accepted my moods, those small, nameless highs and lows I can't always explain. Sometimes I don't say anything at all, but you still notice. In our whole class, you're the one who can tell whether I'm truly happy or just pretending. You never pry. You never try to "fix" me. You're simply there. And when I'm ready to talk, you listen. I've been able to tell you things I could never say to anyone else for fear of being laughed at or misunderstood. With you, they're just my words.

There are many things I'd like to tell you, but most of them are best left to the kind of quiet understanding we've always had. So I'm putting this here, in an appendix that might, to anyone else, read as a series of small, unremarkable memories.

I do know this: in the moments we sat in that pavilion during PE, walked slowly around the track, or when you pressed the pedals and played an imperfect but whole piece — I felt lucky to have you as a friend—or, deeper than that, as my confidante.

I can't promise what the future holds, or that our paths won't diverge. But I can say that however long you walk beside me — through the pocket park, through a sun‑lit track or a shadowed one, through these strange, lengthy pages — I'll be grateful. Grateful for six years of companionship, for everything you've taught me about patience and acceptance, and for the fact that someone like you crossed my path.

With steady friendship,
Yours, who remains deeply thankful for those moments
