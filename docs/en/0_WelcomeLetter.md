To Whom It May Concern

— Being an Epistolary Address to the Young Graduate, on the Eve of a New and Unwritten Age

It was the best of times, it was the worst of times — though those words were written of a distant revolution, they fit your own case with a curious and rather startling accuracy. For you have just closed one book, and now another lies open before you, its pages blank, its margins wide, its ink not yet dry. And between these two volumes — between the last examination and the first independent click of a mouse — there stands a machine.

A laptop. A computer. A box of glass and silicon and quiet, humming mystery.

Perhaps it arrived wrapped in cardboard and bubble‑wrap, a gift from parents who smiled as you tore the tape away. Perhaps you saved, week after week, from part‑time wages or New Year’s money, until the price became a number you could bear to part with. Perhaps it came second‑hand, passed down from an older cousin who said, with the solemnity of a prophet, “She’s got a few more years in her.” However it came to sit upon your desk, it is now yours. Yours alone.

And you have not the faintest idea what to do with it.

Do not blush at this confession. Do not lower your eyes. Every person who has ever touched a keyboard — every engineer, every designer, every gamer who has ever shouted at a lagging screen — was once exactly where you are now. They stared at the unfamiliar glow and felt the same small, honest fear: What if I break it?

Let me tell you a secret. You will not break it. Not truly. Not in any way that cannot be undone. Modern machines are built with the patience of saints and the forgetfulness of goldfish. They have been dropped, drenched, buried under laundry, and still they rise again. The worst you can do — a mistaken deletion, a misplaced folder, a toolbar that multiplies like rabbits — is a small and fixable thing. There are no final mistakes here. There is only discovery, and the gentle art of learning what that curious button does.

For eighteen years, you have lived in a world where questions came with answer keys. The teacher asked, the textbook replied, and the exam measured how well you remembered. But this machine has no answer key. It has no final exam. No one will stand over your shoulder and tell you that you clicked the wrong icon. The only authority in this new world is you.

That is a terrifying thought. And also a glorious one.

I wrote this guide because I remember the first time I held my own computer. I remember the weight of it, the smell of new plastic, the way the screen reflected my own uncertain face. I remember the voice that whispered, What if I break it? And I remember the quiet afternoon, weeks later, when I fixed something that I had broken — and felt, for the first time, not like a user, but like a person who understood.

This book is not a textbook. You need not read it from the first page to the last, nor memorise its passages, nor fear its footnotes. Skim it. Leap about. Read the parts that speak to your present trouble — the file that will not open, the Wi‑Fi that will not connect, the question that you cannot quite put into words. Set it down when you grow weary, and take it up again when curiosity returns. I have tried to write as I would speak to a friend sitting across a table: plainly, sometimes bluntly, but always with the hope that you will leave with more than you brought.

You will find here recommendations for software that does not steal your attention, systems for organising your digital life so that you are not forever searching, explanations of the words that sound like spells but are merely tools. You will find warnings against the traps that caught me and a thousand others — the unnecessary optimisations, the predatory downloads, the beliefs that sound wise but are only old. And you will find, if you look, a few invitations to wander down paths that lead to unexpected places.

One last thing before you begin your journey.

You are leaving a world where most questions had a single, correct, right‑hand‑column answer. The world inside this machine has many answers. Many tools. Many ways to accomplish the same small task. I will show you the ways that worked for me and for the thousands who came before. But you will, in time, find your own. That is not a failure of this guide. That is its only real purpose.

So, dear graduate — dear friend I have never met — take a breath. The screen is dark, but it will not stay so. Place your fingers on the keyboard. Press the power button. Hear the fan begin its quiet whir.

You have finished one chapter. Now another begins — unwritten, ungraded, and entirely yours.

— A fellow traveller, who once stood where you now stand
